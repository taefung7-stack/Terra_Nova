@CH 1
Human beings have inhabited the earth with all kinds of
animals for a long time. Since people in different parts of
the world experience the animal kingdom in their own
environments, they perceive and treat animals differently.
For instance, rats are commonly seen as something to be
avoided in many societies, whereas they are considered
holy in Indian culture. As a matter of fact, there is
even an unusual temple for rats in India where tens
of thousands of rats welcome visitors. Monkeys
present another cultural difference. In Spanish
culture, monkeys are animals to be adored because
they are considered pretty. However, in German and
American cultures, monkeys are often associated
with qualities such as ugliness and foolishness.
@CH 2
Human perceptions of animals indeed differ from culture
to culture. Such dissimilar perceptions are clearly mirrored
in human languages, particularly in the form of animal-based
idioms. Just like other idioms that can pose difficulty for
language learners, animal idioms can also be a potential
source of difficulty. However, not all animal-
related idioms are equally challenging. Some
of them can be easily understood
based on common sense and
general knowledge. Take, for
example, the idiom "like a
crocodile in a wallet factory,"
commonly used in Puerto Rico.
How would a crocodile feel if
it were waiting to be killed and
skinned for wallets? It would
definitely feel "very nervous,"
and that is exactly what the
idiom means. What about
idioms such as "to be like a fish
in water" and "to be like a fish out of water," which are used in
Korea, as well as in Spain, Germany, and many other Western
countries? You can guess their meanings just as easily—to feel
secure and insecure, respectively.
@CH 3
However, there are many animal-related idioms from around
the world whose meanings are quite hard to guess. These
idioms can be confusing to language learners because the
same animal can stand for different ideas. Let us consider
rats first. In Germany, Spain, and the USA, the idiom "to be a
rat" is used to refer to someone who is dishonest, someone
who is extremely stingy, and someone who runs away from a
possibly dangerous situation, respectively. Another example
that shows cultural diversity is the idiom "to be a chicken." In
many Western cultures, chickens often stand for someone who
is cowardly or afraid of taking action. In the USA, the idiom
"chicken out" is often used to describe someone who intends
to do something but eventually does not do it out of fear.
In Egypt, however, chickens are perceived to be useful and
valuable, and thus "to have chickens" means "to have a secure
income."
@CH 4
In many cases, the same idea or quality is expressed using
idioms containing different animals as well. For instance,
stubbornness is expressed by donkeys in German, mules in
English, and cows in Korean. Forgetfulness, which is another
common human trait, is portrayed using various animal
metaphors in different cultures. In Spanish, "to have the
memory of a fish" means that someone is forgetful, while "to
have a bird's brain" conveys the same idea in the USA. Human
beings sometimes disguise themselves to appear nice or kind.
In Japan, "to wear a cat on one's head" means "to pretend to
be nice with one's true self hidden," while in many Western
cultures "a wolf in sheep's clothing" describes someone who
has a friendly appearance, hiding their true harmful intentions.
@CH 5
Some idioms include not just one animal but two.
They often reflect the kind of relationship the animals
maintain in the natural world. For example, Americans
say "to play cat and mouse with someone" to mean
'to attempt to defeat someone by tricking that person
for personal gain'. Both animals also appear in German
and Spanish idioms, symbolizing hostility between
two people. In Korea, on the other hand, a similar
idea is expressed through the idiom "a dog-and-
monkey relationship." The idiom describes a
hostile relationship between two individuals,
similar to the perceived hostility between dogs
and monkeys in the natural world.
@CH 6
You may wonder how all these idioms came into being. In
many cases, it is next to impossible to trace and explain their
exact origins. There are interesting ideas and guesses, though.
Why don't you try to figure out how the Swedish idiom "there is
no cow on the ice" came to mean "there is no need to worry"?
